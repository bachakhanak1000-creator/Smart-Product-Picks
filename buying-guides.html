How to choose wireless headphones

Spec sheets make every pair of headphones look impressive. In practice, four things determine whether you'll still be wearing yours in six months:

  • Comfort over raw sound quality. A pair that clamps too tight or gets hot after an hour will end up in a drawer, no matter how good it sounds in a five-minute store demo. If possible, try before you buy, or buy from a retailer with an easy return policy.
  • Battery life should match your actual routine, not a marketing number. If you forget to charge things, look for 25+ hours of ANC-on playback so a missed night doesn't leave you without sound the next day.
  • Adaptive vs. fixed noise cancellation. Adaptive ANC adjusts strength based on your environment (louder on a plane, lighter in a quiet office); fixed ANC applies the same cancellation everywhere, which some people find creates ear pressure indoors.
  • Multipoint Bluetooth matters if you regularly switch between a laptop and a phone — without it, you'll be manually re-pairing throughout the day.

What to look for in a power bank

Capacity (mAh) is the number brands advertise loudest, but it's rarely the number that matters most:

  • Output wattage determines real-world charging speed. A 20,000mAh bank with a weak 10W output will charge a phone slower than a smaller bank with 20W+ output. Check the output spec, not just capacity.
  • Match capacity to your actual use case. A slim 10,000mAh bank covers one to two phone charges and fits in a pocket. A 20,000–26,000mAh bank is better for multi-day trips or charging a laptop, but is noticeably heavier.
  • USB-C input/output is increasingly the standard — it lets you use one cable for the bank, your phone, and often your laptop charger.
  • Pass-through charging (charging the bank and a device from it at the same time) is a convenience feature not every bank supports — check the spec sheet if that matters to you.

How to choose a USB-C charger

Wattage is the headline spec, but compatibility and safety certifications matter just as much:

  • Match wattage to your fastest-charging device, not your phone alone. A charger rated for a laptop (65–100W) will also charge a phone, but a phone-rated charger (20–30W) won't fast-charge a laptop.
  • Look for Power Delivery (PD) and/or Programmable Power Supply (PPS) support if you own newer phones — these standards negotiate the correct voltage/current automatically.
  • GaN (gallium nitride) chargers run cooler and are usually smaller than older silicon-based chargers at the same wattage, which matters if you're packing it for travel.
  • Multi-port chargers split their total wattage across ports when charging more than one device at once — check the per-port rating, not just the combined total.

How to choose a laptop accessory (mouse, hub, or dock)

The right accessory depends less on brand and more on how you actually work:

  • For mice: if you switch between multiple computers, look for multi-device Bluetooth pairing with a quick-switch button rather than re-pairing manually each time.
  • For USB-C hubs/docks: confirm your laptop actually supports the video resolution and refresh rate advertised — the hub's HDMI spec is a ceiling, not a guarantee, and depends on your laptop's own video output capability.
  • Check pass-through power delivery wattage on hubs — an underpowered hub can bottleneck how fast your laptop charges while connected.
  • Port count vs. desk clutter is a real trade-off — buying more ports than you'll use just adds a bigger footprint on your desk.

What to consider before buying a smart-home device

Smart-home purchases are easy to regret once you realize a device only works well within one ecosystem:

  • Check voice-assistant compatibility first (Alexa, Google Assistant, Apple Home) if you already own smart speakers — mixing ecosystems can mean juggling multiple apps.
  • Hub-based vs. Bluetooth/Wi-Fi-direct devices. Hub-based systems (like Zigbee-based bulbs) generally offer more reliable range and automation but require buying a separate hub. Direct Wi-Fi/Bluetooth devices are simpler to set up but can be less responsive at range.
  • Local control vs. cloud dependency. Some devices keep working (for basic functions) if your internet goes down; others stop responding entirely without cloud connectivity. This is worth checking if reliability matters to you.
  • Firmware update history is a reasonable proxy for how long a manufacturer will keep supporting a device — newer product lines from established smart-home brands tend to get longer support windows.
